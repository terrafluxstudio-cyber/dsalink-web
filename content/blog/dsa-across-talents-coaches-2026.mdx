The DSA-Sec results are in. Over the past two weeks, confirmed offers and wait-list letters have landed in inboxes across Singapore, and by Friday 28 August the window had closed on this year's cohort. For those families, a chapter that opened with open houses back in the first half of the year is finally over.

But results season has a quieter second audience. This is the time of year when parents of younger children start paying attention. A neighbour's daughter gets into her first-choice school through dance. Someone at work mentions their son is training for a sport trial next year. And a question you'd never taken seriously starts to sit at the back of your mind: could this be a path for us?

If that's where you are, you have the one thing this year's applicants no longer do. Time. And time, it turns out, is exactly what the coaches wish more families used well.

Over the past year we put the same three questions to coaches across a wide range of DSA talents. Three of the answers we got back could hardly have come from more different worlds: a badminton academy that sends children to sport trials, a Bharatanatyam teacher who prepares dancers for classical Indian dance auditions, and a debate academy that coaches speech and argument. Different halls, different skills, no contact with one another. Yet they kept circling the same four ideas. When three people in unrelated fields tell you the same thing without ever comparing notes, it's usually worth writing down.

## 1. Stop cramming, and stop hunting for the "perfect" item

As a trial or audition gets close, the instinct is almost always to add. One more session. One more private lesson. One more pass over the single thing your child is going to present. All three coaches, in their own words, said that's the wrong reflex.

The badminton academy was blunt about it. Families load up on private technical lessons in the final weeks and neglect the thing that actually gets tested: real match play, and how a child holds up under pressure. As they put it, "In a crowded hall, selectors aren't just watching a drill — they're watching how a child handles unforced errors, tactical adjustments, and tight scores during actual sparring." You cannot manufacture that in a fortnight of drills.

The Bharatanatyam teacher, Vidhyalakshmi, sees the pressure take a different shape. As the audition nears, parents start asking around: what item did that child perform, why was this one selected, what worked last year. She understands the impulse, but she's watched it backfire. "A Thillana or any other item that worked very well for one student may not be the best choice for another," she wrote. Her advice is to stop shopping for a winning routine and sit down instead, parent, student and teacher together, and look honestly at what this particular child does well. "The focus should not be on finding the so-called perfect item or copying what someone else did."

Even in debate, where there's no physical routine to over-rehearse, the same principle holds. The academy told us the strongest applicants are "strong debaters who can think on their feet, logically respond to a rebuttal, and structure an argument under pressure." A polished, pre-written answer is the opposite of that.

Three completely different talents, one shared warning: preparation is not a last-minute pile-on, and it is not a hunt for one perfect thing.

## 2. A solid foundation beats surface polish

So if cramming is the wrong move, what's the right one? Every coach came back to fundamentals, the unglamorous base a panel can see straight through to.

In badminton, that's court coverage and footwork, and match experience built over years rather than weeks. Inter-school games, the National School Games, the ordinary grind of competing. Their honest read is that the strongest candidates have been playing seriously for a while, not since the DSA deadline first appeared on the calendar.

Vidhyalakshmi was specific about what a dance panel actually watches, and it isn't the one item in isolation. It's the base underneath it: "a strong base in adavus, araimandi, posture, rhythm, coordination and control," carried alongside confidence, expression and musicality. When that base is genuinely solid, she says, "it usually shows even when the student is asked to learn something new or respond to something outside the item they have prepared." Polish sits on top of a foundation. It can't stand in for one.

In debate, the foundation is a real command of English and the ability to build an argument that holds its shape. A child can sound fluent for ninety seconds. The base is what keeps them standing when the questions get harder.

## 3. They are watching how your child takes a correction

Here's the part families least expect. The panel isn't only scoring the routine in front of them. They're watching how a child responds, live, to something nobody rehearsed.

The badminton academy calls it coachability. In their words, schools want players who "demonstrate quick footwork recovery after a shot, fight for every shuttle regardless of the score, and actively absorb advice given during break timeouts." That last part is quietly the most important. A panel can tell the difference between a child who nods politely and one who actually changes what they're doing.

The dance teacher describes the same quality from the inside. When the fundamentals are truly internalised, a dancer can be handed new choreography or asked to improvise and not come apart. Adaptability isn't a separate gift. It's what a solid foundation makes possible.

Debate makes it the most visible of the three. You get handed a rebuttal you didn't see coming, and the whole test is whether you can think instead of recite. In every one of these rooms, what's being measured is flexibility, not a fixed performance.

## 4. The child has to understand, and enjoy, what they're doing

The last thing they agreed on is the hardest to fake, and often the thing that separates an audition a panel remembers from one that was merely correct.

Vidhyalakshmi put it better than we could. A child should be able to talk about their own training, their item, and why they love the dance, comfortably and in their own words. And the memorable dancers, she says, are the ones in whom "the training has become part of the person." Her line stayed with us: the panel should be able to see "the student as a dancer, not simply as someone who has practised one item very well for an audition." A child who is only reproducing remembered steps, she says, tends not to leave the same impression.

The debate academy said something that rhymes with it. The goal isn't a child parroting grown-up opinions. It's a child forming "balanced, well-reasoned views" on issues they can genuinely reach: improving their school, community work, the things a primary-schooler actually cares about and can argue with conviction. Real understanding beats a borrowed talking point every time.

Even in badminton, where you'd think it comes down to pure output, the tell is investment. A child who fights for every shuttle whatever the scoreline is showing something you can't drill into them. Panels notice the kid who plainly wants to be there.

You can coach technique. You cannot coach this. It grows out of a child spending real time on something they actually care about, long enough that it becomes theirs.

## And it starts earlier than the panic suggests

Notice how little of this can be done in a rush. The badminton academy suggests starting around Primary 3 or 4, which gives a child two to three years to build fundamentals and gather match experience before the Primary 6 trials. Most school debate teams, Dialogic noted, start selecting from around Primary 4, so being ready by then is the natural checkpoint, not being a prodigy at Primary 2. Starting earlier isn't essential. It just gives a child, in their words, "more runway to build these skills at a low-pressure pace rather than cramming them in right before trials."

That's not meant to alarm a family only starting to look. It's meant to settle one. The work these coaches describe is the patient, ordinary kind, and if you're reading this early, you have room to do it properly.

## If you're just starting to watch

This year's cohort has its answers now. If you're at the other end, with a younger child and a talent you're only beginning to take seriously, the reassuring thing is how plain the advice turned out to be. A badminton coach, a classical dance teacher and a debate coach, working in three separate worlds, told us the same four things: don't cram or chase a perfect item, build a real foundation, stay coachable, and make sure your child genuinely understands and enjoys what they're doing.

Whatever your child loves, that's the work.
