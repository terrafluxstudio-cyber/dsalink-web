Some parents suspect their child may have ADHD, dyslexia, autism or another condition — but hold back from seeking an assessment, worried that a formal diagnosis will end up on a record and quietly hurt their child's Direct School Admission (DSA) application, or their standing at a preferred secondary school.

It's a worry worth taking seriously, because acting on it has a real cost: a child who could benefit from support goes without it. So it helps to check the worry against what the Ministry of Education (MOE) and the Singapore Examinations and Assessment Board (SEAB) actually publish.

## What a DSA application actually shares with schools — and what it doesn't

When you apply through the [DSA-Sec process](/dsa-guide), MOE lists what goes in: your choices of school and talent area and, optionally, up to 10 non-school-based awards, activities and achievements related to your child's chosen talent areas.

Separately, your child's primary school information is automatically shared with the DSA schools you apply to. MOE states this includes P5 and P6 academic results, and P4–P6 information such as CCA participation, Values in Action involvement, school-based achievements and awards (for example Edusave awards), National School Games participation, Junior Sports Academy participation, and National Physical Fitness Award results (for sports talent).

Look at what is on that list: academic results, talents and school-based activities. A medical report or diagnosis is not among the things a DSA application submits or automatically shares. (The School of the Arts and the Singapore Sports School handle their own direct admission and sit outside this shared-information arrangement.)

## Does a diagnosis help or hurt? What an assessment unlocks

Here's the part worried parents often don't realise: getting your child assessed helps them — it does not count against them. It matters in two different settings.

**In school, day to day.** MOE says mainstream primary schools support students with conditions including attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, dyslexia, mild autism spectrum disorder, and hearing, visual and physical impairments. Support can include in-class help and small-group intervention from SEN Officers, the Learning Support Programme (Primary 1–2), Learning Support for Mathematics (Primary 1–4), dyslexia remediation, and TRANSIT for behavioural support. Schools identify students who need help through, in MOE's words, "information gathered from parents and teachers, and through systematic observations conducted by trained school personnel" — so school-based support does not always require a formal diagnosis. MOE also suggests that parents who are concerned "seek a professional assessment."

**In national examinations.** For the PSLE and later national exams, SEAB grants Access Arrangements to candidates with special educational needs so they are not unfairly disadvantaged by standard conditions. For the PSLE these can include about 25% extra time, a separate or isolation room, enlarged (A3) print, additional preparation time for oral examinations, a word processor or a reader pen. Here a formal report matters: SEAB requires "a clear statement of diagnosis of a Physical or Learning Disability" from qualified professionals, and for some arrangements a current profile of need dated within three years of the exam year. Approval is common — SEAB has said that of about 6,000–7,000 Access Arrangement applications each year across the PSLE and GCE N/O/A-Level examinations, about 99% were approved over the past five years.

One practical note, per SEAB: for the PSLE, a school candidate's application is made through the school (speak to the SEN officer or Access Arrangement coordinator), and the deadline is mid-February of the exam year — so an assessment needs to happen well before then.

## What appears on the result slip — and what never does

This is the point parents most often get wrong, so here is the current rule, precisely.

When Access Arrangements are granted, most arrangements — including extra time — carry a neutral symbol next to the relevant subject on the result slip and certificate: a "+" for an access arrangement, or a "#" for an exemption. So it is not accurate to say nothing is ever shown.

But here is what those symbols do not reveal — stated plainly by the authorities:

- **The condition itself is never shown, and never made public.** In SEAB's words: "Details of the AA provisions and the related medical conditions are not indicated on your examination result documents, and SEAB will not share them with members of the public."
- **The symbol does not affect secondary school posting.** In MOE's words: "A student's progression to secondary education will not be affected by the annotation, as school postings do not take such annotations into account."

So even where a symbol appears, it names no diagnosis, discloses no arrangement detail, isn't shared with the public, and isn't used in [posting to secondary school](/dsa-results).

## What this means for parents

Based on what MOE and SEAB publish, the specific fear — that a diagnosis will surface in the DSA process or quietly count against your child — is not supported by the rules:

- a DSA application submits and shares academic results, talents and school activities; a medical report is not among them;
- an assessment is what makes fair exam Access Arrangements possible, and most applications are approved;
- on results, the condition itself is never shown or shared, and posting to secondary school does not take the annotation into account.

None of this is about hiding anything — it's the opposite. The official position is that support exists, and that parents who are concerned should seek a professional assessment. If worry about DSA or secondary school has been the reason to hold off, the published rules should take that particular worry off the table — leaving the decision where it belongs: with your child's wellbeing and learning. Many [real families navigate the DSA journey](/dsa-experience) alongside exactly these considerations.

### A few practical points

- Access Arrangements for the PSLE are applied for through the school, by mid-February of the exam year; leave enough time for an assessment before then.
- Some arrangements require an assessment or a profile of need dated within three years of the exam year.
- School-based support does not always require a formal diagnosis; national-examination Access Arrangements do.
- Policies can be updated — confirm current details with your child's school, MOE and SEAB for your specific case.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do secondary schools see my child's medical report in a DSA application?**
No. A DSA application submits your school and talent-area choices and up to 10 talent-related achievements, and it automatically shares P5–P6 results, CCA, Values in Action, school-based awards, National School Games, Junior Sports Academy and NAPFA (for sports talent). A medical report or diagnosis is not among them.

**Does a diagnosis appear on the PSLE certificate?**
Access Arrangements may carry a neutral symbol (+ or #), but SEAB states the medical condition and arrangement details are never indicated on result documents and are not shared with the public.

**Will access arrangements affect Secondary 1 posting?**
No. MOE states a student's progression to secondary education is not affected, as school postings do not take such annotations into account.

**What access arrangements can a child get for the PSLE?**
Examples include about 25% extra time, a separate room, enlarged print, a word processor or a reader pen. A diagnosis report is required, and about 99% of Access Arrangement applications are approved each year. Applications are made through the school by mid-February.

**Do I need a formal diagnosis for my child to get support in school?**
Not necessarily. Schools identify needs through parent and teacher input and trained observation, so school-based support does not always require a diagnosis. National-examination Access Arrangements do require one. MOE suggests concerned parents seek a professional assessment.
